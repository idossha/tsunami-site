Why Tsunami

The objective

To make the practice that is awkward on a computer easy on the device in your pocket. Much of what the sciences of medical-school admission ask is a picture, a formula or a process: where a structure sits, how a relation is written, what happens in what order, what a pathway looks like. Typing a word into a box, or flipping a card and judging yourself, practises none of that well. A finger does.

Tsunami is one person's study tool, made for the exam its author is preparing for and shared because it may be useful to others. It promises no score.

The ideas it is built on

Answer the way the knowledge is shaped

A figure is answered by tapping the figure, a formula by building it, a process by ordering its steps, a structure by drawing it. Each kind of card has one way to answer, and it is the same every time, so attention goes to the question and not to the controls. See the five kinds.

Recall first, then an explanation, every time

You answer before you see anything. Then the back explains the answer, the same way whether you were right or wrong: a right answer may have been a guess, and a wrong one needs the reason more than the mark. Every option of a choice says why it is right or what makes it wrong.

A path, not a pile

The built-in deck is 114 units in teaching order, from the basics up. A unit first teaches its terms and then asks you to use them in a case the textbook did not give. A drawing is asked for only where the answer really is a drawing, and only after the cards that teach its parts.

Little and often

Reviews are scheduled with FSRS, the spaced-repetition algorithm Anki also offers: a card comes back shortly before you would forget it. New cards arrive up to a daily limit you set, so a day's work stays small enough to do every day.

Nothing to trust blindly

Every card names the textbook section it comes from. Every card is written by one reader and checked against that section by a second reader who did not write it, and a card changed after its check is checked again before it ships. Every picture shows who made it and under which licence. The sources are on the credits page.

Yours, and quiet

No account, no ads, no purchases, no analytics, no reminders. The app works offline and your progress is a file on your device that you can export and restore. The privacy policy is short because there is little to say.

What it leaves out, on purpose

  • Plain text flashcards. Anki does those well; Tsunami is for what Anki does poorly on a phone.
  • Writing decks in the app. A deck is a file in an open format; you can open your own, made on a computer.
  • Streak pressure and notifications. Statistics show what you did; nothing nags.
  • A predicted score. The app does not know what you will get, so it does not say.

How it is made

The cards are written from openly licensed textbooks, most of them from OpenStax, and the deck is free under the same kind of licence, which is also why the app is free and will stay so. The scheduling is tested against the reference implementation of FSRS, and every screen is tested on an iPhone, a small iPhone and an iPad before a build goes out.

Tsunami is in private beta. Take the tour, or report a wrong card or a bug.

Tsunami is free: no ads, no purchases, no account, and nothing is collected.
